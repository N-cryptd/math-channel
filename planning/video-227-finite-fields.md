## Competitive Analysis

**Key competitors:**
1. **Michael Penn** -- 'Finite Fields' (~30-50K views). Whiteboard, covers existence/uniqueness, Frobenius, examples. Dense, no animation.
2. **Dr. Peyam** -- 'Finite Fields' (~15-25K views). Conversational, good examples, covers structure theorem.
3. **Borcherds** -- brief mention in broader field theory lectures.
4. **Mathologer** -- 'Galois Theory' touches on finite fields as examples.

**Market gap:** No animated video systematically covers finite fields with the structure theorem, Frobenius automorphism, and multiplicative group cyclicity. We fill this.

**Our positioning:** The final theory video before the summary. Finite fields are incredibly clean and well-structured, providing a satisfying contrast to the complexity of the Abel-Ruffini proof.

**Techniques to adopt:**
- Build GF(p^n) explicitly for small cases (GF(4), GF(8), GF(9))
- Show the Frobenius automorphism as a permutation of field elements
- Visual: show the multiplicative group as a cyclic arrangement

**Techniques to avoid:**
- Full existence proof via splitting fields (state, motivate, cite)
- Overloading with coding theory or cryptography applications
- Skipping the uniqueness theorem (existence + uniqueness is the key result)

## Scene Plan (8 scenes)

### Scene 1: Hook -- The Simplest Nontrivial Field (55s)
**Content budget:** intro + title + 3 progressive items
**Narration (~28s):** "We've studied field extensions over Q and C. But there's a completely different world of fields: finite fields. The simplest is Z over pZ for p prime, with p elements. But there are more: for every prime power p to the n, there exists a unique field with p to the n elements. These finite fields have a remarkably clean structure that connects Galois theory, number theory, and applications in coding and cryptography."

- play_intro("Finite Fields", "Advanced Abstract Algebra")
- Title: "Fields with Finitely Many Elements"
- 3 progressive items:
  1. "Z/pZ for p prime: fields with p elements"
  2. "For every prime power p^n: a unique field with p^n elements"
  3. "Remarkably clean structure: Galois theory in miniature"

### Scene 2: The Prime Field (55s)
**Content budget:** section divider + title + definition + examples
**Narration (~28s):** "Every field has a smallest subfield, called its prime field. The characteristic of a field is the smallest positive n such that n times 1 equals 0. If no such n exists, the characteristic is 0, and the prime field is Q. If the characteristic is p, a prime, then the prime field is Z over pZ. The characteristic of a finite field must be prime, because if characteristic were composite n equals ab, then a times b equals 0 in a field, which contradicts the field axioms."

- Section divider: "1 -- Basics"
- Title: "Characteristic and Prime Field"
- Definition: char(F) = smallest n with n*1 = 0
- Key: char(F) is always 0 or a prime
- Examples: char(Q) = 0, char(Z/pZ) = p

### Scene 3: Existence and Uniqueness (80s)
**Content budget:** section divider + title + theorem box + construction
**Narration (~40s):** "The fundamental theorem of finite fields: for every prime p and positive integer n, there exists a finite field with p to the n elements, and it is unique up to isomorphism. The construction uses splitting fields. The polynomial x to the p to the n minus x has p to the n distinct roots in some extension of Z over pZ. These roots form a field, which we call GF of p to the n, or the Galois field. The uniqueness follows from the fact that any field with p to the n elements consists exactly of the roots of x to the p to the n minus x."

- Section divider: "2 -- Existence and Uniqueness"
- Title: "The Fundamental Theorem of Finite Fields"
- Theorem box: For each prime p, integer n >= 1, there exists a unique (up to iso) field with p^n elements
- Construction: roots of x^{p^n} - x in an extension of F_p
- Notation: GF(p^n) or F_{p^n}

### Scene 4: The Frobenius Automorphism (75s)
**Content budget:** title + definition + properties + examples
**Narration (~38s):** "Every finite field has a canonical automorphism: the Frobenius map, F, which sends x to x to the p, where p is the characteristic. This is a field automorphism because it preserves addition by the binomial theorem and clearly preserves multiplication. Over GF of p to the n, the Frobenius has order n, meaning applying it n times gives the identity. The Galois group of GF of p to the n over GF of p is cyclic of order n, generated by the Frobenius. This is perhaps the cleanest example of a Galois group."

- Title: "The Frobenius Automorphism"
- Definition: F(x) = x^p
- It is a field automorphism (binomial theorem)
- F has order n on GF(p^n)
- Gal(GF(p^n)/GF(p)) = <F> is cyclic of order n

### Scene 5: The Multiplicative Group (70s)
**Content budget:** section divider + title + theorem + examples
**Narration (~35s):** "The multiplicative group of a finite field is cyclic. That is, GF of p to the n star is cyclic of order p to the n minus 1. This is a theorem whose proof uses the structure theorem for finite abelian groups. A primitive element of GF of p to the n is a generator of this cyclic group. For example, GF of 4 has multiplicative group of order 3, which is cyclic. And GF of 9 has multiplicative group of order 8, which is cyclic. Finding a primitive element in practice often requires computation."

- Section divider: "3 -- Structure"
- Title: "The Multiplicative Group Is Cyclic"
- Theorem box: GF(p^n)* is cyclic of order p^n - 1
- Notion of primitive element (generator)
- Examples: GF(4)* ≅ C_3, GF(9)* ≅ C_8

### Scene 6: Subfields and the Galois Correspondence (70s)
**Content budget:** title + correspondence + example
**Narration (~35s):** "The subfields of GF of p to the n correspond bijectively to the divisors of n, via the Galois correspondence. The subfield corresponding to d dividing n is the fixed field of F to the d, and has p to the d elements. For example, GF of p to the 12 has subfields corresponding to divisors 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 12. The subfield for d equals 4 has p to the 4 elements and is the fixed field of the Frobenius to the 4th power."

- Title: "Subfields of GF(p^n)"
- Correspondence: divisors d | n <-> subfields of order p^d
- The subfield for d is Fix(F^d)
- Example: subfields of GF(p^12) correspond to d in {1,2,3,4,6,12}

### Scene 7: Applications (55s)
**Content budget:** title + 3 items
**Narration (~28s):** "Finite fields are essential in modern applications. In coding theory, Reed-Solomon codes use arithmetic in finite fields to detect and correct errors. In cryptography, the Diffie-Hellman key exchange and elliptic curve cryptography work over finite fields. In combinatorics, finite projective planes are constructed from finite fields. These applications alone would justify studying finite fields, but their mathematical beauty is the real reason."

- Title: "Applications"
- 3 progressive items:
  1. "Coding theory: Reed-Solomon codes"
  2. "Cryptography: Diffie-Hellman, elliptic curves over finite fields"
  3. "Combinatorics: finite projective planes"

### Scene 8: Summary and Outro (50s)
**Content budget:** section divider + title + 5 takeaways + outro
**Narration (~28s):** "Let's recap. Finite fields exist for every prime power order and are unique. The Frobenius automorphism generates the Galois group, which is cyclic. The multiplicative group is cyclic. Subfields correspond to divisors via the Galois correspondence. And the applications span coding, cryptography, and combinatorics. This completes our tour of advanced abstract algebra."

- Section divider: "4 -- Summary"
- Title: "Key Takeaways"
- 5 progressive items:
  1. "GF(p^n) exists and is unique for every prime p, n >= 1"
  2. "Frobenius F(x) = x^p generates Gal(GF(p^n)/GF(p))"
  3. "GF(p^n)* is cyclic of order p^n - 1"
  4. "Subfields of GF(p^n) correspond to divisors of n"
  5. "Applications: error-correcting codes, cryptography, combinatorics"
