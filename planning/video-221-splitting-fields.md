## Scenes (8 scenes)

### Scene 1: Hook — "Where Do the Missing Roots Live?" (20s narration)
- **Content budget:** Title + 3 bullet points
- Start with x^2 - 2 = 0. Over Q, it has no root. Over R, it has sqrt(2) and -sqrt(2).
- x^3 - 2 = 0 over Q is even worse: needs cube root of 2 AND complex cube roots.
- The splitting field is the answer: the smallest field containing ALL roots.
- **Visual:** Polynomial shown, then roots "appearing" one by one with color coding.
- **Narration:** ~60 words (~25s)

### Scene 2: Definition — What is a Splitting Field? (24s narration)
- **Content budget:** Section title + boxed definition + notation + 1 remark
- Formal definition: f(x) in K[x] splits over E if f(x) = c * prod(x - r_i) with all r_i in E
- Splitting field of f over K: the smallest extension E/K where f splits
- Notation: Split_K(f) or just "the splitting field"
- Key remark: the splitting field is the field K(r_1, ..., r_n) generated by all roots
- **Visual:** Factorization animation — polynomial breaking apart into linear factors
- **Narration:** ~70 words (~29s)

### Scene 3: Existence — Every Polynomial Has a Splitting Field (24s narration)
- **Content budget:** Section title + theorem statement + construction steps (3 items)
- Theorem: Every polynomial f(x) in K[x] has a splitting field over K
- Construction idea (not full proof):
  1. Pick an irreducible factor g(x) of f(x)
  2. Adjoin a root alpha of g to K via K[x]/(g(x))
  3. Over K(alpha), f(x) has one more linear factor
  4. Repeat until fully split
- The process terminates because deg f is finite
- **Visual:** Tree diagram showing K → K(alpha_1) → K(alpha_1, alpha_2) → ...
- **Narration:** ~70 words (~29s)

### Scene 4: Uniqueness — Up to Isomorphism (22s narration)
- **Content budget:** Section title + theorem + explanation (3 items)
- Theorem: Any two splitting fields of f over K are isomorphic
- Why it matters: the Galois group Aut_K(E) is well-defined regardless of which splitting field we pick
- Proof idea: extend isomorphisms one root at a time (isomorphism extension theorem)
- **Visual:** Two different-looking field diagrams connected by a double-headed arrow (isomorphism)
- **Narration:** ~65 words (~27s)

### Scene 5: Example 1 — x^2 - 2 over Q (26s narration)
- **Content budget:** Section title + polynomial + factorization + degree computation + remark
- f(x) = x^2 - 2 is irreducible over Q (Eisenstein with p=2)
- Adjoin sqrt(2): Q(sqrt(2)) contains both sqrt(2) and -sqrt(2)
- Splitting field = Q(sqrt(2)), [Q(sqrt(2)) : Q] = 2
- Tower: Q ⊂ Q(sqrt(2)), degree 2
- **Visual:** Number line showing Q and the two roots appearing; nested circle diagram
- **Narration:** ~78 words (~32s)

### Scene 6: Example 2 — x^3 - 2 over Q (30s narration)
- **Content budget:** Section title + roots + tower diagram + degree computation + note
- Roots: cuberoot(2), omega * cuberoot(2), omega^2 * cuberoot(2) where omega = e^{2 pi i / 3}
- Step 1: Adjoin cuberoot(2). [Q(cuberoot(2)) : Q] = 3 (irreducible by Eisenstein)
- Step 2: omega not in Q(cuberoot(2))! Need to adjoin it. [Q(cuberoot(2), omega) : Q(cuberoot(2))] = 2
- Splitting field = Q(cuberoot(2), omega), degree = 3 * 2 = 6
- Tower: Q ⊂ Q(cuberoot(2)) ⊂ Q(cuberoot(2), omega), degrees [3][2] = [6]
- **Visual:** Tower diagram with degree labels, complex plane showing the three roots
- **Narration:** ~90 words (~37s)

### Scene 7: Degree Bounds and Key Properties (24s narration)
- **Content budget:** Section title + 3 properties
- Theorem: [Split_K(f) : K] divides n! where n = deg f
- Corollary: If deg f = n, the splitting field has degree at most n!
- Example: For x^3-2, degree is 6 which divides 6 = 3!
- The Galois group Gal(Split(f)/K) is a subgroup of S_n
- **Visual:** Formula boxed, then the S_n connection teased
- **Narration:** ~70 words (~29s)

### Scene 8: Summary and Teaser (28s narration)
- **Content budget:** Section title + 4 takeaways + outro
- Key takeaways:
  1. Splitting field: smallest extension where f splits completely
  2. Exists for every polynomial, unique up to isomorphism
   3. Degree divides n! and can be computed via tower law
   4. The Galois group acts on the splitting field (next video!)
- Teaser: "The Galois group of f is the group of automorphisms of the splitting field that fix K. This is where the magic happens."
- Play outro
- **Narration:** ~85 words (~35s)
